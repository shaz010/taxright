W-2 Employment Income ?
📄
What is a W-2?
It's a form your employer sends you every January. It shows how much you earned and how much tax was already taken out of your paychecks. Think of it as your job's report card for the IRS!
📍 Where to find it: Check your work email in January, your company's HR portal, or your mailbox. Had two jobs? You'll have two W-2s — enter both!

Investment & Passive Income
From 1099-DIV, 1099-INT, 1099-B, and Schedule K-1 forms.
💰
Ordinary Dividends
When you own stock or mutual funds, companies sometimes share their profits with you — that's a dividend! This is the total amount they paid you this year.
📍 Where to find it: Box 1a on your 1099-DIV (emailed or mailed in January by your brokerage).
⭐
Qualified Dividends
Some dividends get a special tax break and are taxed at a lower rate than regular income. This number is always smaller than or equal to your ordinary dividends above — don't add them together!
📍 Where to find it: Box 1b on the same 1099-DIV form. It's already included in Box 1a.
⚡
Short-Term Capital Gains
If you sold a stock, crypto, or other investment that you owned for less than one year, your profit (or loss) goes here. The IRS treats this just like a paycheck — taxed at your regular rate.
📍 Where to find it: Your brokerage's 1099-B form or year-end statement. Look for "short-term." Can be negative if you lost money!
📊
Long-Term Capital Gains
If you owned an investment for more than one year before selling, you get a tax break! The IRS rewards patient investors — you'll pay 0%, 15%, or 20% instead of your regular tax rate, which is usually lower.
📍 Where to find it: Your brokerage's 1099-B form. Look for "long-term." This is one of the best tax breaks available!
🔄
Capital Loss Carryforward
If you lost more than you gained from investments in a previous year, the IRS lets you carry those unused losses into this year. It's like a coupon that reduces your taxable gains!
📍 Where to find it: Last year's Schedule D (capital gains worksheet) from your prior tax return. Leave blank if this is your first year investing or you had no prior losses.

Social Security Benefits
If you receive Social Security payments, you might owe a little tax on part of them — but usually not all of it! If Social Security is your only income, you probably owe nothing. If you have other income too, up to 85% may be taxable.
📍 Where to find it: Box 5 on your SSA-1099 (the pink Social Security statement mailed each January). Only enter the amount in Box 5, not Box 3 or 4.

IRA / 401k Withdrawals
If you took money out of a retirement account (IRA, 401k, 403b, etc.) this year, that counts as income and you'll owe tax on it. This is the taxable portion — usually the full amount unless you made non-deductible contributions before.
📍 Where to find it: Box 2a on your 1099-R (mailed by your retirement plan in January). If Box 2a says "Unknown," use Box 1 (the total distribution).
⏰
Early Withdrawal Penalty
The IRS really wants you to keep retirement money for retirement! If you took money out before age 59½, there's usually an extra 10% penalty on top of the normal tax. However, there are exceptions — like if you were disabled, bought your first home, or set up equal regular payments.
📍 How to check: Look at Box 7 on your 1099-R. Code "1" usually means the penalty applies. Code "2" means you qualify for an exception. Not sure? Check the instructions on the back of your 1099-R.

Business Expenses ?
🗂️
Business Expenses (Schedule C)
Every dollar you spend on your business can reduce your taxes! The rule is simple: the expense has to be "ordinary" (normal for your type of work) and "necessary" (helpful for earning income). Always keep receipts — the IRS can say "no" if you can't prove it.
📍 Good rule of thumb: Save every receipt for anything over $25. Take photos with your phone right away so you don't lose them. Your bank and credit card statements are great backup proof.
Enter annual totals for each category. Leave blank if not applicable.
🍽️
Business Meals (50% Rule)
Taking a client out to lunch? Eating while traveling for business? You can deduct half! The IRS lets you write off 50% of business meal costs. Just enter the full amount you spent — we'll automatically calculate the 50% for you.
📍 What counts: Meals with clients, customers, or business partners where business was discussed. Keep the receipt and note who was there and what you talked about on the back!
🤝
Payments to Contractors
If you hired freelancers or independent contractors to help with your business, you can deduct what you paid them! Important: if you paid any single person $600 or more, you're required to send them a 1099-NEC form by January 31st.
📍 What to enter: Total paid to all contractors combined. If you use a payment platform like PayPal or Venmo for business, check if they already sent a 1099-K — don't count it twice!
💻
Equipment & Depreciation
Bought a computer, camera, tools, or other equipment for your business? Great news — you can often deduct the full cost in the same year you bought it (no waiting!). This is called Section 179. For 2025, you can deduct up to $1,250,000 in equipment right away.
📍 What counts: Computers, phones (business %), cameras, machinery, office furniture — anything with a useful life over one year used for your business. Keep your receipt or invoice showing the date and price.

Home Office Deduction ?
🏡
Home Office Deduction
If you work from home and have a space used only for business, you can deduct a portion of your home costs (rent, mortgage, utilities, internet)! The key rule: the room or area must be used exclusively for work — no bed in the corner, no watching TV there.
📍 Two methods: Simple = $5 per sq ft (max 300 sq ft = $1,500 deduction, no math needed). Actual = track all home costs and multiply by office percentage. Most people use Simple — it's easy and usually close to the same!

Vehicle & Mileage Deduction
Drive your car for work? Every business mile saves you money! In 2025, the IRS lets you deduct 70¢ for every business mile driven. Commuting to your regular office doesn't count, but driving to a client, the post office, or a job site does!
📍 What you'll need: Your total business miles for 2025 and your odometer reading on Jan 1 and Dec 31. Keep a simple mileage log — even a note in your phone with date, destination, and miles. The IRS loves a good log!

Deductions & Adjustments

We'll compare standard vs. itemized and pick what saves you more.

Above-the-Line Adjustments ?
📐
Above-the-Line Adjustments
These are extra special deductions because they reduce your income before anything else is calculated — even if you're not itemizing! Think of them as VIP discounts that everyone gets to use, no matter how you file.
📍 What qualifies: Student loan interest, IRA contributions, health savings accounts (HSA), self-employed health insurance, and retirement plans for self-employed people are all listed below.
🎓
Student Loan Interest
Paying off student loans? You can deduct up to $2,500 of the interest you paid this year — even if you don't itemize! This is free money that many people miss. (Note: this benefit phases out for higher earners.)
📍 Where to find it: Box 1 on Form 1098-E, sent by your loan servicer (Sallie Mae, Navient, etc.) in January. You can also find it in your loan servicer's online portal.
🏦
Traditional IRA Contribution
A Traditional IRA is a retirement savings account where your contributions may be tax-deductible right now — meaning every dollar you put in could lower your tax bill today! You can contribute up to $7,000 (or $8,000 if you're 50 or older) and have until April 15, 2026 to contribute for 2025.
📍 Where to find it: Your brokerage or bank statement showing your 2025 IRA deposit. Double-check you labeled it as a "2025 contribution" when you made it.
🏥
Health Savings Account (HSA)
An HSA is like a special piggy bank for medical bills — money goes in tax-free, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for healthcare! To contribute, you must have a High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). It's one of the only accounts with a triple tax benefit!
📍 Where to find it: Your HSA provider mails Form 5498-SA showing your contributions. Or look at Box 12 Code W on your W-2 for employer contributions (don't enter employer amounts here — they're already not taxed).
🩺
Self-Employed Health Insurance
If you're self-employed and pay for your own health insurance (not through an employer), you can deduct 100% of those premiums! This includes medical, dental, and vision insurance for yourself and your family. This is a huge benefit that employed workers don't get.
📍 What to enter: The total premiums you paid in 2025 for health, dental, and vision coverage. Check your insurance statements or bank records for the amounts. Don't include months where you were also covered by a spouse's employer plan.
💼
SEP-IRA / Solo 401k
Self-employed people can save a lot more for retirement than regular employees! A SEP-IRA lets you set aside up to 25% of your profit (max $69,000) and deduct every dollar. A Solo 401k works similarly. Both are great ways to legally lower your tax bill while building your future.
📍 Where to find it: Your SEP-IRA or Solo 401k account statement showing 2025 contributions. You have until your tax filing deadline (with extensions) to make SEP-IRA contributions for 2025!

Mortgage Interest
The interest you pay on your home loan is often tax-deductible! This is one of the biggest deductions homeowners have. The government helps make homeownership affordable by letting you deduct this from your income.
📍 Where to find it: Box 1 on Form 1098 (Mortgage Interest Statement) — your lender mails this in January. Only the interest portion counts, not the principal (the part that pays down the loan).
🏛️
Property Tax
The property tax you pay on your home can be deducted! However, there's a combined limit of $10,000 total for all state and local taxes (property tax + state income tax combined). This is called the SALT cap.
📍 Where to find it: Your county tax bill or mortgage escrow statement. Your lender may also include it on Form 1098. Enter the amount you actually paid in 2025, not the amount billed.
🗺️
State & Local Income Tax
You can deduct state income taxes you paid in 2025 — but remember, the total with property taxes is capped at $10,000! If you live in a state with no income tax (like Florida or Texas), you can deduct sales tax instead using IRS tables.
📍 Where to find it: Your state tax return shows how much you paid. Or check your pay stubs — it shows state tax withheld each pay period. Add up all state tax withheld for the year.
❤️
Cash Donations to Charity
Money donated to qualified charities (churches, non-profits, food banks, etc.) can be deducted! The IRS rewards generosity. Just make sure you have proof — a bank statement, cancel check, or receipt from the charity.
📍 Important: For any donation of $250 or more, you need a written "thank you" letter from the charity confirming the amount and that you received nothing in return. Most charities send these automatically at year-end.
👕
Non-Cash Donations (Clothes, Goods)
Donated bags of clothes to Goodwill? Dropped off furniture at Salvation Army? You can deduct the fair market value of what you gave — what it would sell for at a thrift store, not what you originally paid. Take photos of everything before you donate!
📍 What you need: A receipt from the charity listing items donated. For donations over $500 total, fill out Form 8283. For items worth over $5,000, you'll need a professional appraisal. Apps like ItsDeductible can help estimate values.
💊
Medical & Dental Expenses
If your out-of-pocket medical bills were really large this year, you might be able to deduct them — but only the amount that exceeds 7.5% of your income. For example, if you earned $50,000, the first $3,750 of medical costs don't count, but anything above that does!
📍 What counts: Doctor bills, prescriptions, dental, vision, hearing aids, hospital stays — expenses you paid out of pocket (not reimbursed by insurance). Enter your total and we'll calculate the deductible portion automatically.

Child Tax Credit ?
👶
Child Tax Credit
Having kids saves you money on taxes! For each child under 17 at the end of 2025, you get a $2,000 tax credit — and up to $1,700 of that can come back to you as a refund even if you owe no tax! We'll calculate this automatically from the dependents you entered in Step 1.
📍 What qualifies: Your child must be under age 17 on December 31, 2025, live with you for at least half the year, and have a Social Security number. You don't need to do anything extra — just make sure you added them in Step 1!

Child & Dependent Care Credit ?
🧒
Child & Dependent Care Credit
Pay for daycare or after-school care so you could work? The government helps! You can get back 20–35% of what you spent on childcare (up to $3,000 for one child, $6,000 for two or more). This applies to kids under 13, or any dependent who can't care for themselves.
📍 What counts: Daycare centers, family daycare, after-school programs, and summer day camps. Does NOT include overnight camps or school tuition (kindergarten and above). Both you and your spouse must have earned income or been full-time students.
🏫
What Childcare Costs Qualify?
Enter the total you paid in 2025 for care while you worked. Daycare, after-school programs, babysitters (even a teenager you hire), and summer day camps all count. Overnight summer camps and school tuition (K-12) do NOT count.
📍 Where to find it: Check your bank statements or childcare provider receipts. Your daycare may have sent you Form 2441 or a year-end statement showing total amounts paid. Ask your provider for their Tax ID number — you'll need it!

Retirement Savings Contribution Credit ?
🐷
Saver's Credit
This is a special bonus for low-to-middle income earners who save for retirement! If you contributed to an IRA, 401k, or similar account, you might get an extra credit worth 10–50% of what you saved (up to $1,000, or $2,000 for couples). It's free money for doing the right thing!
📍 Income limits for 2025: Single filers earning under $39,500 qualify. Married couples earning under $79,000 qualify. If you're near these limits, it's still worth entering your contributions — you might qualify for a partial credit!

📅
Estimated Tax Payments
If you're self-employed (or had side income with no withholding), you're supposed to pay taxes four times a year — kind of like a paycheck, but you send the check to the IRS yourself. These are called "estimated payments." If you made them, enter them here so you get credit!
📍 Where to find them: Check your IRS.gov account, your bank statements for payments to "US Treasury," or your Form 1040-ES payment voucher stubs. The four due dates in 2025 were April 15, June 16, September 15, and January 15, 2026.
